# Scientific Research Design

Use to formulate a scientific problem, choose the research scope, establish
a testable objective, or assess whether a proposed model/method/system
actually answers the stated question. The output is a **research contract**,
not a manuscript section or an invented discovery.

## Before work

1. Identify the discipline, intended contribution, research object,
   measurable property, intended readers, scope and known constraints.
2. If a degree, program, journal or grant imposes a research-area
   classification, read its current **official** scope and explain the
   substantive fit of the problem and proposed result to relevant
   points. Do not infer fit from a topic word, named technique or hardware.
   Project-specific classifications never become universal requirements.
3. Establish the authoritative current proposal and prior decisions,
   including what remains a hypothesis. Do not overwrite an approved
   direction merely to make the narrative seem more novel.

## Problem-to-proof chain

Construct and inspect this chain, with evidence for each connection:

**Problem and observed limitation → objective and measurable criterion →
hypothesis/model → method and executable algorithm (where applicable) →
evaluation protocol → result → qualified conclusion.**

- The objective identifies **what changes or is established and how to
  evaluate it**. "Develop and study" describes an activity, not success.
- Define inputs, outputs, assumptions, domain, constraints and controls.
  For an algorithm, specify operational steps, branches, edge conditions
  and stopping rules; a component diagram alone is not an algorithm.
- Distinguish abstract method, mathematical model, algorithm,
  implementation architecture and one specific test configuration.
  A parameter of a test rig need not be universal to the method;
  a parameter that determines behavior must be defined in the method.
- Distinguish theoretical proof, numerical model, simulation,
  implementation, system experiment and operational deployment.
  Each answers a different question; no single green check substitutes
  for every evidence level.
- When comparing approaches, determine common baselines, metrics and
  input conditions *before* reporting improvements.

## Research inventory and handoff

Record one row per proposed result: scientific problem; claimed
contribution; closest known alternatives; criterion; planned evidence;
observed evidence and status; limitations and next experiment.
Statuses should distinguish **proposed**, **supported by literature**,
**implemented**, **experimentally checked**, and **not checked**.
The table organizes work; filling a row does not certify novelty.

If the problem has no accessible evidence, specify the missing source,
dataset, baseline, or experiment, and continue only independent valid
work. Never fill missing results with plausible numbers. A theoretical
study need not invent hardware tests, and a replication or negative
result need not pretend to introduce a new method.

Use separate Skills for focused prior-art/novelty checking, literature
synthesis, experimental reproducibility, and manuscript preparation
only when the task requires them. Do not load a whole dissertation
workflow for an ordinary scientific design question.

**Completion:** each claimed objective has an evaluation route and
clear evidence status; unresolved assumptions are visible.
